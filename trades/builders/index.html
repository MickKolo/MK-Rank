Builders get researched, not just called

Building work covers everything from a garden wall to a two-storey extension, and the way people search changes with the size of the job.

Big projects

Extensions, loft conversions, garage conversions and full renovations. Homeowners spend weeks looking, often searching the job and the town together, such as "extension builders Poole". They want to see similar projects, read reviews and know you'll still be around next year.

Smaller jobs

Repointing, garden walls, patios, structural repairs and knock-throughs. Decisions are quicker, so the map pack, your reviews and how fast you answer matter more than a long portfolio.

Trade and commercial work

Architects, developers, managing agents and businesses. They usually hear about you first, then search your name. Your website needs to show capability, insurance and accreditations, not just pretty photos.

Project pages and photos that rank

For a builder, the best content you'll ever have is your own work. But a gallery of forty photos with no words doesn't rank for anything, because Google can't tell what it's looking at. Writing up your projects turns that work into pages that do.

  • A page for each main service. Extensions, loft conversions, renovations, new builds. Each one says what's involved, how long it usually takes and what you need from the customer.
  • Short project write-ups. What the customer wanted, where it was (town or area), what you did and how long it took, with before, during and after photos. A page like that can rank for "single-storey extension Broadstone".
  • Photos that describe themselves. Give each photo a sensible file name and a short description, such as "rear kitchen extension with bifold doors, Wimborne", so search engines know what it shows.
  • Proof you finish jobs. Completion photos and, where the customer agrees, a quote from them. Unfinished-looking galleries put people off.

Reviews that describe the job

A builder finishes far fewer jobs a year than a plumber or electrician, so you'll never have hundreds of reviews. That makes each one count for more. Fifteen detailed reviews about extensions in your area are worth more than fifty that say "great job".

The best moment to ask is at handover, when the customer is walking round the finished room. Send your review link the same day, and ask them to mention what you built and where. Reply to every review, and keep the same business name on your Google profile, Checkatrade and anywhere else you're listed.

A good builder's review mentions

  • The type of project, such as a loft conversion
  • The town or area
  • Keeping to time and budget, or how changes were handled
  • A tidy site and good communication

Trust signals: FMB, TrustMark and insurance

Customers handing over tens of thousands of pounds want reassurance before they sign. Most builders have the right credentials but bury them in the footer. Put them where people are deciding:

  • Memberships and schemes. If you're a member of the Federation of Master Builders or registered with TrustMark, show the logo near your contact details and link it to your listing so customers can check it.
  • Insurance and guarantees. Say that you carry public liability insurance, and explain any warranty or insurance-backed guarantee you offer, in plain words.
  • Building control and planning. Explain who handles building regulations and planning on your jobs. It's one of the things homeowners worry about most.
  • Commercial accreditations. Health and safety schemes such as CHAS or SafeContractor matter to commercial and public sector clients, so give them a line on your commercial page.
  • How long you've traded. If it's been twenty years, say so near the top of the home page.

Builder SEO questions

I only want bigger projects. Can SEO help with that?

Yes, that's one of the best reasons to do it. Build your pages around extensions, loft conversions and renovations, put your best projects front and centre, and keep small repairs to a line on your services page. Google and customers both take their cue from what you give the most space to.

How do I show my projects without giving away customers' addresses?

Name the town or area, never the street. Ask the customer's permission before you publish, and crop out house numbers and car number plates. Most customers are happy to say yes when they're pleased with the work, especially if you offer to leave their name off.

Should my Google profile show my yard or office address?

Only if customers visit it. If they don't, hide the address and set a service area instead, which is what Google expects for builders who travel to the job. If you do have a yard or office where people can drop in during staffed hours, showing it can help you rank nearby.

I get most of my work from architects and referrals. Is SEO still worth it?

It's worth getting the basics right. Referrals still search your business name before they call, and an architect sending a client your way wants to see a professional website and recent reviews. Beyond that, local SEO lets you add direct enquiries on top, so you're not waiting on someone else's pipeline.
